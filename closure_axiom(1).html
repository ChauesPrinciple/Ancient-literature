Sacred Mathematics

The organizing idea: the sacred cannot leak, so the space it inhabits is finite, closed, and without boundary

What this page argues

This is a genealogy of technique. It is not a claim that three cultures independently discovered the same manifold. The śulba sūtras (Vedic India, roughly 800 to 500 BCE) give rope-and-peg constructions for fire altars and state the relation we call the Pythagorean theorem, centuries before Pythagoras. Whether those texts contain proofs in the Greek sense, or statements with constructions that assume them, is a live question among historians of mathematics. Al-Bīrūnī (Islamic Persia, 973 to 1048 CE) carried triangle geometry onto the surface of the sphere, calculating the qibla, the direction of Mecca, from any point on Earth. Dante (1265 to 1321 CE) worked in a culture that had inherited both, and described a cosmos in Paradiso that a twentieth-century physicist identified as a 3-sphere.

The tools accumulate. That is the claim, and the eleven panels below run in that order: a closure argument, then the triangle, then the sphere, then the 3-sphere and Dante's route through it, and last the question the geometry raises about freedom.

The geometry of Paradiso

In 1979 the physicist Mark Peterson argued that Dante's cosmos, as described in Paradiso XXVIII, is a 3-sphere. The geocentric universe from Earth out to the Primum Mobile forms one hemisphere. The Empyrean, with its angelic spheres contracting toward a luminous point, forms the other. They join at the equator of the Primum Mobile. Peterson's conclusion was that if the passage is not describing the organization of 2-spheres into a 3-sphere, it is hard to see what it is describing. Dante himself believed he was saying something new.

The mathematician John Baez frames the same structure as a one-point compactification of three-dimensional Euclidean space. Add a single point at infinity, and that point is God, the punto. Satan sits at the origin, the farthest point from it. Barsella and Vespri (2021, with a synthesis for Digital Dante in 2022) proposed a refinement using a stereographic model, grounded in Dante's own image of the universe as pages bound into a single volume, legato con amore in un volume / ciò che per l'universo si squaderna (Par. 33, 86 to 87). Each S² slice is a page and the cosmos is the book. Stereographic projection was available in Dante's period, so this is not modern mathematics imposed on a medieval poet.

Why closure

A boundary would be an edge with nothing on the other side, and nothing to explain why the edge falls where it does. That dissatisfaction is old, and Leibniz gave it its most precise formulation four centuries after Dante in the Principle of Sufficient Reason: nothing happens without a reason why it should be so rather than otherwise. Leibniz is a later articulation of the intuition, not its source, and he cannot be Dante's reason for anything. The same principle applied to physics produced the variational tradition, in which light takes the path of least time and matter the path of stationary action. The geodesic, the shortest path on a curved surface, is where this page's geometry and that tradition meet, and it is what panel 9 draws.

In 1922 Pavel Florensky was the first to read the Commedia through non-Euclidean geometry, proposing that Dante's passage through Satan's body, where down becomes up, is a change of sheet on a Riemann surface. Florensky was working before Peterson and reached the problem from a different direction; how far the two readings agree is a question for Dante scholarship rather than something this page settles. What is modeled here is Peterson's S³.

Plato's Timaeus (around 360 BCE) assigned the dodecahedron, the most sphere-like of the five regular solids, to the cosmos itself. The Pythagorean Tetractys, 1+2+3+4 = 10, structures the poem: three cantiche of 33 cantos each, plus the opening canto of the Inferno, giving 100, which is 10 squared.

PART IClosure

Why a finite space can have no edge, argued first where it is easiest to see.

1. The five closures: Platonic solids

What this shows. Only five convex regular polyhedra exist. The constraint (r−2)(n−2) < 4, where n is the number of sides of each face and r the number meeting at a vertex, admits exactly five solutions. This is a closure argument of the same family as the boundary argument for S³: only these configurations close. Plato's Timaeus assigned four to the elements and reserved the dodecahedron for the cosmos. Euler's formula V−E+F = 2 holds for all five, which is the topological signature of a closed surface. Kepler nested all five between the planetary orbits in the Mysterium Cosmographicum (1596).

PART IIThe triangle, and the sphere it is drawn on

2. The sacred triangle

What this shows. The spherical triangle whose vertices are the North Pole, an observer, and Mecca. The śulba sūtras made the right triangle the basis of sacred construction. Al-Bīrūnī carried the triangle onto the sphere to solve the qibla. The figure is laid out to the true side lengths and the true angles, and it redraws as you move the observer in panel 3.

The drawing is planar, so its three angles must sum to 180 degrees while the true spherical angles sum to more. That difference is the spherical excess, and on a unit sphere it equals the triangle's area. It is the reason a flat map of the sky always distorts something, which is what panel 4 is about.

4. The astrolabe: stereographic projection as a thousand-year-old machine

What this shows. Projection from the south celestial pole onto the plane of the equator, which is what an astrolabe's plate is. Ptolemy's Planisphaerium describes it; al-Bīrūnī wrote on astrolabe construction. Stereographic projection is conformal: it preserves angles. The cross drawn at the star marks two directions that are perpendicular on the sphere, and the readout measures the angle between their images on the flat plate. It stays at 90 degrees wherever you put the star. That is the property that makes the plate a true map of the sky's angles, and it is why the same projection can carry Dante's cosmos in panel 10.

PART IIIThe three-sphere

One more dimension of the same move, and the route Dante takes through it.

5. The ladder: S⁰ to S³

What this shows. How spheres build from zero dimensions to three. Each step adds one angular coordinate and closes the space further.

6. Dante's S³, every named level

What this shows. Hell's nine circles descend from Earth's surface to Satan, the spheres shrinking. Purgatory's seven terraces rise from the surface toward the Moon. Nine celestial spheres grow from the Moon to the Primum Mobile at ψ=π/2, the largest S² in the cosmos. Above it nine angelic orders shrink toward God. Both poles are points. That inversion is what Peterson identified in Paradiso XXVIII. Widths here are the honest sin ψ.

7. The journey, and why down becomes up

What this shows. The whole route as one continuous path. Depth is ψ: Satan at 0, Earth's surface just above, the Primum Mobile at π/2, God at π. Horizontal position is where you stand on the S² at that depth. Left of the axis is Jerusalem's hemisphere. Right is the antipodal hemisphere, where Mount Purgatory stands.

The inversion. At ψ=0 the sphere is not small, it is a single point. A path continuing straight through it has nowhere to come out except the antipode. Dante and Virgil never turn around; the sphere runs out from under them, and the angular coordinate flips by π. That is the whole content of down becoming up, and it is why the descent is drawn on the left and everything after Satan on the right. Florensky located the critical moment exactly here.

9. The geodesic: a great circle on S³

What this shows. γ(t) = cos t · u + sin t · v, with u and v orthonormal. Every such path is a great circle, the shortest route between any two of its points, and it closes. Along this one x₃ = cos t · cos τ, so ψ runs over [τ, π−τ] and the tilt slider sets exactly how deep the path can reach.

The finding. Hell occupies ψ < 0.15, which is 8.6 degrees. A geodesic reaches Hell only if its plane lies within 8.6 degrees of the polar axis. Tilt past that and the path, still a perfect great circle and still the shortest route between its own points, never touches the lowest circles. Closure does not guarantee that a path passes through everything the space contains.

PART IVWhat the geometry costs

If the whole of a life can be seen at once, what is left to decide?

11. The ladder of vision

A Square lives in a plane. He moves freely in two directions, and the third is hidden from him not by distance but because he has no faculty that reaches it. When a Sphere passes through his world he sees a circle that grows, shrinks and vanishes. From above, the Sphere sees the whole plane at once, including the inside of A Square's house and the inside of A Square.

We are in that position with respect to time. We occupy four dimensions and perceive three of them, one slice at a time, in an order we cannot reverse. The next slice is hidden from us the way the third dimension is hidden from A Square. Every rung below is a question about a viewer, not about how many axes the world happens to have.

Three scenes cross-fade as the slider moves. First, an agent at the present with three axes of free motion and a fan of possible futures dissolving into fog. Second, a single world-line along a time axis from birth to death, watched from above. Third, a branching tree of possible worlds with one path picked out.

The tradition's answer

The ladder poses a threat. If the whole of a life can be seen at once, and every alternative to it seen alongside, what is left to decide? It is worth knowing that the writers usually named on this question all faced it, and all answered that nothing is lost.

Boethius, in the last book of the Consolation, argues that God's knowledge is not foreknowledge at all. It is an eternal present, and seeing does not cause. His image is the survey from a lofty height: God looks forth at all things as from a peak, and the act of vision adds no necessity to what it sees, just as your seeing a man walk does not make his walking necessary. Aquinas later refined this into the road-and-travellers form, a man in a high tower seeing every traveller at once, but the argument is Boethius's.

Leibniz builds his distinction between the necessary and the contingent for exactly this purpose. A truth is necessary when its denial is a contradiction; contingent truths are certain without being necessary. On his account the survey of all possible worlds leaves them contingent. He is the last person who would say the third rung abolishes choice. He is also the same Leibniz whose Principle of Sufficient Reason opens this page, which is worth noticing: the principle that closes the cosmos is not the principle that closes the will.

Dante holds the same position and gives it to Marco Lombardo, who insists that the heavens begin our motions without determining them, and that free will survives.

So the ladder is a question the Commedia is already answering. The poem is not adjacent to this dispute. It is inside it, and the reader has to decide whether the answer holds.

Citation note: the road-and-travellers form of the watcher image is Aquinas's refinement (in his commentary on Aristotle's De Interpretatione 9) of Boethius's "lofty height" survey in Consolation V.6; the page now attributes it accordingly. Marco Lombardo's free will speech is Purgatorio XVI.67-78 (core: "Lo cielo i vostri movimenti inizia" at line 73, "e libero voler" at line 76); the broader speech runs to line 145.
